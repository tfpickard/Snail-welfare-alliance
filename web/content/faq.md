# FAQ

*Pre-empting the objections — answered directly, without hedging or moralizing.*

---

**The numbers are tiny. Why bother with this when billions of food animals suffer?**

Scale doesn't determine whether an act is wrong — it determines how many times it's wrong. The Tyrian purple case is worth working on precisely *because* of its small scale: the harm is unnecessary, the alternative is trivial, and abolishing the practice requires almost nothing from anyone. That's rare. We're not arguing this is the most important animal welfare cause. We're arguing it's one of the most *winnable*, and that winning it makes the broader case that invertebrate welfare is worth taking seriously.

---

**You don't even know if snails feel pain.**

We don't. We state that openly. Here's what we do know: gastropods have nociceptors, an endogenous opioid system with a μ-opioid receptor ~95% identical to ours, and documented long-term nociceptive sensitization. Their molluscan relatives — cephalopods — are now legally recognized as sentient in the UK and EU. The biology is suggestive enough that the precautionary argument holds: given that the killing is entirely unnecessary, there is no cost to stopping.

---

**What about the Mexican tradition? Aren't you attacking indigenous culture?**

The opposite. The Mixtec dyers of Pinotepa de Don Luis, Oaxaca have practiced non-lethal "milking" of *Purpura pansa* for approximately 3,000 years — pressing the living snail to release its secretion, then returning it alive to the rocks. This tradition is protected under Mexican law, celebrated by us, and is our primary proof of concept. Purple does not require death. It never has. The lethal practice is a Western industrial shortcut, not a universal or traditional one.

---

**What about religious use? The tzitzit tradition is sincere.**

We respect it and say so explicitly. Ptil Tekhelet's work represents a sincere effort to fulfill a religious obligation, and we don't attack that. Our position is narrower: the identical molecule is available synthetically, the Tekhelet tradition itself acknowledges the dye is visually indistinguishable from indigo, and — if the goal is to fulfill the religious commandment — a synthetic 6,6′-dibromoindigo should be acceptable on the merits. We promote substitution; we don't demand it.

---

**Isn't the "natural is better" argument valid? Connoisseurs say they can tell the difference.**

No. Natural and synthetic Tyrian purple are the same molecule. 6,6′-dibromoindigo is 6,6′-dibromoindigo regardless of how it was produced. Any perceived difference between natural and synthetic dye baths reflects impurity composition and bath chemistry — both factors that are fully engineerable synthetically. The "natural is superior" claim is a connoisseurship mythology, not a chemical fact.

---

**Why not target factory farming instead? The scale comparison makes this look absurd.**

Because we exist specifically to work on this. Different causes benefit from different campaigns. The Shrimp Welfare Project doesn't campaign against factory farming — it works on shrimp. We work on murex snails. These things aren't competing; they're complementary. If the answer to "don't do this unnecessary thing" is always "but what about the bigger thing," nothing ever gets fixed.

---

**What is Snail Welfare Alliance?**

Snail Welfare Alliance (SWA) is an open coalition of researchers, designers, donors, and advocates working to end the lethal extraction of Tyrian purple from murex snails. The org name reflects the model: this isn't a top-down charity asking for sympathy — it's a coordinated effort across backgrounds to make one specific, winnable argument land. Our flagship campaign is "Where Are the Snails?" The merch line is "Save the Snails."
