# The Facts

*A complete, sourced account of Tyrian purple and why we're here.*

---

## I. What Tyrian purple is

Tyrian purple — also called imperial purple, Phoenician purple, or *purpura* — is one of the oldest and most storied colorants in human history. For roughly three millennia, from ancient Phoenicia through the Byzantine empire, it was extracted from predatory sea snails in the family Muricidae: principally *Bolinus brandaris* (the spiny dye-murex) and *Hexaplex trunculus* (banded dye-murex), with a related New World species, *Purpura pansa*, used by Mesoamerican dyers.

The compound responsible for the color is **6,6′-dibromoindigo**, a brominated indigo derivative. It is stable, light-resistant, and saturated — properties that made it uniquely valuable before synthetic chemistry existed. In antiquity it was worth approximately three times its weight in gold (Diocletian's Edict on Maximum Prices, 301 CE).

---

## II. How it is still made today

Tyrian purple dye production did not end with antiquity. It persisted in small, specialist, and religious contexts, and underwent a revival in the late 20th century. Today, at least four production streams exist.

**Tunisia.** Mohamed Ghassen Nouira spent approximately fourteen years reverse-engineering the Phoenician extraction process. He sources *Bolinus brandaris* at the La Goulette fish market in Tunis, cracks each shell by hand, removes the hypobranchial gland — the organ that produces the dye precursor — and processes the secretion into pigment. He has produced several dozen grams over his entire career. He sells at approximately $2,500 per gram.

**Israel.** Ptil Tekhelet, based at Kfar Adumim, is the largest organized producer of natural Tyrian purple in the world. It processes approximately 400,000 snail glands per year, producing roughly 20,000 sets of ritual *tzitzit* (fringes worn under Jewish law). Each set requires approximately 30 snails. The organization has dyed over 270,000 sets to date. The snails are *Hexaplex trunculus*, sourced partly as a byproduct of the commercial seafood industry. All extraction is lethal. We note this context: the *tekhelet* commandment is a sincere religious obligation, and Ptil Tekhelet's work represents a good-faith effort to fulfill it. Our position is that a synthetic substitute of identical composition is available and should be considered.

**Japan.** Textile artist Masakazu Akiyama (Aya studio) and researcher Takako Terada have revived shellfish purple dyeing in Japan using lethal gland extraction. Akiyama's dyed pieces have been presented to the Japanese Imperial Household.

**Mexico.** The Mixtec dyers of Pinotepa de Don Luis, Oaxaca use a **non-lethal** method: the living snail (*Purpura pansa*) is pressed gently against the thread, causing it to release its secretion, and then returned alive to the rocks. This technique has been practiced continuously for approximately 3,000 years. Approximately 13–15 elderly licensed dyers remain. The practice is regulated by the Mexican government since 1988, following near-collapse caused by commercial over-exploitation in the early 1980s. This is proof that Tyrian purple does not require death.

## III. The numbers

**Per unit:**
- ~10,000–12,000 snails per gram of dye
- ~12,000 snails for a single ancient garment trim ("half a thimbleful")
- ~30 snails per ritual tzitzit fringe set

**Global annual dye-kill:** No precise figure exists for active publication. A defensible order-of-magnitude estimate — based on Ptil Tekhelet's reported volume and other known producers — is **under 1 million snails per year specifically for dye production**. This is a stated estimate, not a verified total.

**Scale in context:**
- Food snails killed annually: 2.9–7.7 billion (Rethink Priorities, 2023)
- Shrimp killed for food: trillions annually
- Dye murex killed: roughly one-millionth of the food-snail total

We state this comparison openly because it is both true and important. The scale of dye-murex killing is negligible by comparison to food-animal production. Our argument does not rest on scale. A needless act of killing a possibly-sentient animal for a luxury color is wrong on a per-act basis. And because the scale is tiny and the alternative is trivially available, abolishing it is the easiest harm-reduction available in the snail-welfare space.

## IV. The biology — what we know and what we don't

We state the following as established:

- Murex snails and other gastropods possess **nociceptors** — neurons functionally analogous to pain receptors.
- Gastropods have an **endogenous opioid system**. The μ-opioid receptor in sea snails is approximately **95% identical** to the human μ-opioid receptor in structure.
- In *Aplysia californica* (a sea slug), long-term nociceptive sensitization has been documented that is functionally similar to chronic pain in vertebrates.

We do not state:

- That *Bolinus brandaris*, *Hexaplex trunculus*, or *Purpura pansa* specifically are sentient, or that they consciously suffer. No formal sentience assessment has been conducted for these species.

We note as relevant context:

- Cephalopod mollusks (octopus, squid, cuttlefish) — closely related to gastropods within Mollusca — are now **legally recognized as sentient** under the UK Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022 and under EU regulatory frameworks.
- Decapod crustaceans received similar recognition in the same period.
- The **New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness** (2024), signed by leading consciousness researchers including Christof Koch, Anil Seth, and David Chalmers, affirms a "realistic possibility" of conscious experience in many invertebrates.

Our stated ethical position: **assume a capacity for pain until proven otherwise**. Pain is a primal survival mechanism. The biology is suggestive. The cost of being wrong is borne entirely by the animal. Given that the killing is unnecessary, this precaution has no cost.

## V. The alternative

The same molecule, 6,6′-dibromoindigo, has been synthesizable in a laboratory since **1904**.

Multiple clean, published syntheses now exist. The compound is not chemically exotic — it is a halogenated indigo derivative, related to the common dye indigo in structure.

In **2023**, the biotechnology company **Conagen** announced the first commercial-scale **fermentation-based** production of Tyrian purple, using engineered microorganisms to biosynthesize the molecule. They described it as "democratizing a color once reserved for royalty."

**Natural vs. synthetic — the "it looks better" claim:** Natural and synthetic Tyrian purple are the same molecule. The Tekhelet tradition itself acknowledges that the dye is visually indistinguishable from indigo ("only God can tell them apart"). Any perceived difference between natural and synthetic dye baths reflects differences in impurity composition and bath chemistry — both fully controllable in synthetic production. The claim that natural Tyrian purple is visually superior to its synthetic twin does not survive scrutiny.

## VI. Economics

Current retail price for natural Tyrian purple: approximately €2,562 per gram (Kremer Pigmente, 2024). The market is negligible: a collector and specialist niche with no industrial or fashion relevance. This is not a campaign about an industry — it is a campaign about a custom.

## VII. The argument

1. **Unnecessary.** A synthetic twin of identical composition has existed since 1904 and is commercially available.
2. **Gratuitous.** The killing of ~10,000 animals per gram serves no function that cannot be served by a molecule from a flask.
3. **Per-act wrongness.** We do not require sentience to be proven. The precautionary case is sufficient given what the biology already shows.
4. **Trivial to prevent.** The market is tiny. The ask — "don't buy lethal natural Tyrian purple" — is one of the smallest possible sacrifices.
